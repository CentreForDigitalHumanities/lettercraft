Glossary

In studying narrative descriptions of epistolary communication, the Lettercraft team has identified a large number of Latin words that have omething to do with letters and their use. One substantial segment of this vocabulary refer to letters themselves. Other clusters refer to the people carrying them, to accompanying gifts, and to the wider communicative process in which letters functioned. One of the aims of the Lettercraft project has been to map out this terminology so as to gain a better understanding of its meaning and use. To this end, all episodes in the database contain a ‘designator’ field that gives relevant Latin epistolary terminology that appears in the passage concerned. These terms can be searched with the search function. A full terminology list, with frequencies, can be downloaded for individual texts and for the database at large.

To help navigate this part of the database, we have created a glossary of the most important terms one might encounter. We have divided the glossary in four segments: (1) Letters, (2) Gifts, (3) Messengers, and (4) Actions. The first three segments consist of nouns, the last segment of verbs. For nouns, entries typically give the nominative and genitive singular forms, with the gender specified between brackets, e.g. ēdictum, ī (n.). Plural forms are given instead if the noun occurs mostly in the plural, e.g. apicēs, um (m., pl.). For verbs, we give the present infinitive, followed by the first person singular present, the first person singular perfect, and the supine, e.g. invītāre (invītō, invītāvī, invītātum). NB: we give the infinitive first, rather than the first person present as is customary in Latin dictionaries, because the infinitive form is logged in the database. To aid pronunciation and scansion, we added macrons (ā, ē) to long vowels, following the marking used in the Oxford Latin Dictionary. For the sake of legibility, use of macrons is generally restricted to the lemma header; they are not given in the rest of the entry unless to distinguish certain grammatical forms.

Each entry gives the principal meanings of the term from Antiquity up to the Merovingian period, followed by selected attestations from texts in the database. For further context, entries might also include references to other ancient and medieval sources, and to relevant modern scholarship. For the letter-, gift- and messenger-sections, it has proven insightful to compare the Merovingian narrative corpus to letters, formularies, and legal texts from the period, as these bring to light different patterns of usage. The verb section, by contrast, is limited to examples from the Merovingian narrative corpus. The bibliography at the end offers a full list of the primary sources, dictionaries, and modern publications cited in this glossary.

Lastly, a caveat. Given the many documents, objects, agents and actions involved in Merovingian epistolary communication, the glossary cannot claim to be an exhaustive overview of Merovingian epistolary terminology. That list would be near-endless. As it stands, we have tried to be comprehensive for the Latin terms for letter, at least where their use in the Merovingian world is concerned, while giving the most frequently attested terms for gifts, messengers and actions.
